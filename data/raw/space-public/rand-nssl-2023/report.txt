    The U.S. National Security Space Launch program is responsible for launching the military and
intelligence community satellites that serve as the global eyes, ears, and timekeepers of the nation. In
2019, the U.S. Air Force tasked RAND with providing analysis of the global heavy lift launch market
to inform the Air Force’s National Security Space Launch Phase 2 acquisition. Decisions of foreign
launch providers in the international market and the emergence of new domestic launch service
providers in the years after 2019 may provide opportunities for the U.S. Space Force (USSF) to better

prior analysis. The intended audience for this report includes USSF leadership, the Department of the
Air Force, and the U.S. Congressional Armed Services and Appropriations Committees.

Assured Access to Space and conducted within the Force Modernization and Employment Program
of RAND Project AIR FORCE as part of a fiscal year 2023 project, “Space Launch Market Research

Summary


Issue
    The U.S. military depends on space-based assets for strategic and tactical communications, for
collection of imagery over contested areas, for warning of missile launches, and for precision navigation
and timing. The ability to replenish those assets in a timely fashion may affect the overall readiness
of the force. For this reason, the U.S. Space Force (USSF) is required to sustain at least two viable
U.S.-based launch service providers for national security space (NSS) payloads. In 2019, a RAND
team assessed the global heavy lift launch market to understand the impact of NSS launch acquisition
decisions on that market. Space Exploration Technologies Corporation (SpaceX) and United Launch
Alliance (ULA) are the current NSS launch providers, having been selected for the Phase 2 launch
contracts awarded between fiscal years 2020 and 2024. Although ULA is retiring its prior NSS-
certified launch vehicles (Atlas V and Delta IV Heavy), its new vehicle (Vulcan) has yet to complete
NSS certification, leaving the USSF temporarily reliant on SpaceX as the only NSS-certified launch
service provider. Given events over the past four years, the RAND team was asked to update our
assessment of the launch markets to better understand how the USSF might shape future acquisition
strategies or prepare for operational risks introduced by past acquisition decisions.

Approach
    To conduct the assessment, we examine historic and projected levels of supply and demand in the
global and NSS heavy lift launch markets. Using these data, we forecast demand over the next ten
years (2024–2033) and use Monte Carlo simulations to explore how assumptions regarding future
market dynamics might affect the USSF’s ability to meet NSS demand and sustain at least two
certified suppliers, ideally with support from the commercial markets.

Key Findings
    •   The average yearly global launch demand addressable by U.S. firms is largely unchanged since
        the 2019 assessment; while demand is diversifying (see Figure S.1), we expect to see only slight
        overall growth in the next five years.
    •   There are few viable competitors in the global addressable launch market; SpaceX dominates
        with a 70-percent share of the addressable demand in 2022 (compared with a 40-percent share
        in 2019).
    •   Given the current lack of competitors, several firms are seeking to enter the heavy lift launch
        market; we expect rapid consolidation in the market should any be successful.
    •   Despite the diversity offered by the possible new entrants, there is little diversity in the key
        resources (fuel, launch complexes, and engine suppliers) needed for launch.

collection of imagery over contested areas, for warning of missile launches, and for precision navigation

ULA is retiring its prior NSS-certified launch vehicles (Atlas V and Delta IV Heavy), its new vehicle
(Vulcan) has yet to complete NSS certification, leaving the USSF temporarily reliant on SpaceX as
the only certified NSS launch service provider.3 Given these events and others over the past four years,

Launch Markets
      As in 2019, we recognize two markets in our analysis. The first is the portion of the global heavy
lift launch demand that is addressable by U.S. firms, and the second is the NSS launch market. In the
global addressable market, both supply and demand can be heavily influenced by international events
and by national regulations. A prime example regarding dynamisms of supply occurred at the outbreak
of war between Russia and Ukraine in February 2022. Prior to the outbreak of the war, the Soyuz
launch vehicle (of Russian origin) had a sizable share of the global addressable market. That share
vanished virtually overnight after the head of Roscosmos, the Soyuz supplier, refused to launch a
payload consisting of 36 OneWeb satellites unless the government of the United Kingdom divested
itself of OneWeb (the UK government had invested $500M in OneWeb to bring the firm back from
bankruptcy).12 The UK government did not divest, and OneWeb terminated its remaining contracts
with Soyuz. To complete the build-out of its constellation of satellites, OneWeb awarded space
launch contracts to the Indian Space Research Organisation for its Geosynchronous Satellite Launch
Vehicle (GSLV) and to SpaceX for its Falcon 9. On the demand side of the equation, much of the
total global market is fenced off by various nations in support of their own national goals.
      The NSS launch market is much less dynamic. Demand is often set years in advance and, while it
can fluctuate based on U.S. national security priorities, it is quite stable. Supply has historically been
limited to those launch providers who undergo a lengthy process to become “certified” NSS providers.
This will change under the Phase 3 acquisition, which has partitioned the overall NSS demand into
two lanes. The first is for more risk-tolerant payloads and will award indefinite delivery/indefinite
quantity contracts with no restrictions on the number of providers and minimal qualification
requirements.13 The second lane is modeled after the Phase 2 procurement to assure access to
NSS-certified launch services for NSS high mission assurance payloads.

     It is extremely difficult to enter the launch market, and launch vehicles rarely mature as planned.
As we noted in the 2019 assessment, “Hypothetically, if all of these [new entrant] launch vehicles were
to mature as planned, in 2022 there would be an astounding 19 vehicles competitive in the heavy lift
launch market.”8 However, of the new entrants listed in 2019, only the Falcon Heavy and the Long

years before a vehicle claims a significant share of the global addressable market; the provider is unable
or unwilling to cross the chasm either because the vehicle does not match market need (e.g., the Falcon
Heavy) or because the provider chooses to compete only in domestic markets (e.g., the Long March

enthusiasm needed to mature the IRL is not without risk, however. If the next launch attempt does
not show substantial TRL progress, it could be a death blow to nascent business cases.

missions, it is our assessment, based on discussions with stakeholders, that buyer enthusiasm has
waned. Given that the New Glenn is designed to support established and evolving business cases,
rather than entirely new ways to make money, the lack of public visibility into TRL progress may not
matter to the IRL. As with the Ariane, if New Glenn can be brought to market prior to the other new

Heavy Lift Launch Demand


    Our research seeks to understand how trends in the demand for heavy lift launch affect U.S.
suppliers to that market. Therefore, in this chapter, we update the historical analysis of the global
addressable market for heavy lift vehicles to reflect four years of change and examine the overall trends
to discover possible sources of growth. We then forecast the addressable demand for the next ten
years. We also provide a short recap of NSS demand, to include Phase 2 awards.


Addressable Launch Market Demand
    The size of the addressable heavy lift launch market has been relatively stable over the last
16 years.1 The average has fallen slightly since the 2019 assessment to just 19 launches per year, given
relatively lackluster demand in 2019 and 2020. Demand peaked in 2022 and fell, but remained above
average, in 2023. In the following sections, we plot the demand by year for a variety of categorizations
and examine them for market trends.

satellite missions. While demand from satellite communications service providers fell and remained
flat from 2019 to 2023, the demand from imaging and earth science missions grew, in part due to
more nations establishing space agencies.2 In cases where a nation does not have domestic launch
capability, these national space agencies become a new source of demand within the addressable
market.

     Over the last four years, OneWeb and Starlink completed build-out of their initial constellations,
and existing smaller constellations, such as Iridium NEXT and O3b, began their replenishment
launches. OneWeb survived a period of bankruptcy when its business case failed and is now partially
owned by the UK government. Fewer OneWeb launches were made than originally predicted, as
the firm reduced constellation size and scaled back objectives.5 Starlink has also scaled back plans
(including plans for a constellation in very low earth orbit) and has begun launching its heavier second-
generation Starlink satellites. These heavier satellites reduce the number of satellites that will fit on a
Falcon 9 and has increased the number of launches required to deploy the constellation, increasing
total investment cost.6 To support replenishment and sustainment of the Starlink constellation while
also supporting NSS and addressable demand, SpaceX has increased its Falcon 9 launch cadence to
once a week, with a goal of 100 launches per year.7 However, a lack of subscribers, heavier satellites,
and more launches all negatively affect Starlink’s business viability.8
     We do not anticipate any constellations arising within the next five years to replace OneWeb’s
addressable demand for launch services. Amazon Kuiper and Telesat, two other proposed proliferated
LEO constellations, had nebulous late-2020 initial service dates. Telesat has one functional prototype
satellite in orbit, while Amazon Kuiper is still a concept.9 It is uncertain how sustainable proliferated
LEO constellations are, and this market might collapse if the core business case of supplying high
speed/low latency internet service from space is weaker than firms’ scaled-back plans have accounted
for. As we noted in the 2019 assessment, the difference between the number of “underserved” internet
users and the number of people willing to pay for high-quality space-based internet service may be
much larger than these business models anticipate.10 Higher interest rates for capital may also
adversely affect business models. Based on low subscription and adoption rates to date, the underlying
satellite communications market may not have sufficient demand to generate the revenues needed to
sustain the existing constellations.
     One final question to address before moving on is whether GEO is still a preferred destination for
satellites given the growth of proliferated LEO constellations. Figure 3.2 shows how demand has
varied by destination orbit and clearly shows that destinations have diversified. However, major
providers of satellite communication services (Eutelsat, Inmarsat, Intelsat, and Viasat) continue to
launch new satellites into GEO, committing their resources to this domain. As the relatively small
subscriber base for Starlink services demonstrates, we have not yet witnessed a mass exodus of GEO-
based communications services in response to proliferated LEO alternatives.

A Market Becomes a Monopoly: 2019–2022
    As we noted in the 2019 assessment, there are strong buyer incentives to maintain competition in
the addressable heavy lift launch market, and those market forces were not expected to allow SpaceX’s
Falcon 9 to continue to take a 50-percent market share. In fact, SpaceX’s share dropped significantly
in 2019 and remained down through 2021. The bulk of the shift in market share was to the Soyuz
launch vehicle, which was selected to be a primary provider for the OneWeb constellation, in a deal
brokered by Arianespace. This may have continued into 2022 had Russia not invaded Ukraine. As
shown in Figure 3.3, the Soyuz addressable market share disappeared almost overnight with the
invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, going from nine addressable launches in 2021 (all but one of
which were from OneWeb) to two launches in 2022 (for Angola and Iran, two of the nations who do
not recognize the economic sanctions imposed in the wake of the invasion).11 It is unclear if and when
Russian launch vehicles will return to the global addressable launch market. Prior to February 2022,
Russia hoped to return to full competition with its next-generation launch vehicle, the Irtysh
(Soyuz 5). As of August 2023, Russia plans to debut the Soyuz 5 on December 24, 2025.12 While in
the past Ariane 5 held a significant share of the addressable market, Arianespace launched its final
Ariane 5 rockets in July 2023, bringing a conclusion to its 27-year run.13 Delays have pushed the
maiden launch of its next-generation model, Ariane 6, to 2024.14

Factors Commercial Buyers Consider When Selecting a Launch Service
Provider
      As noted above, the communication service market remains the largest commercial buyer of heavy
lift launches. The business model is characterized by very large up-front capital investments followed
by revenue streams lasting the lifetime of the satellites and generally has a distinct multiyear capital
expenditure cycle. Proliferated LEO constellations to date have had larger capital expenditures than
those needed for the more traditional GEO constellations. While only a few of the proliferated
constellations have begun replenishment, sustaining a proliferated constellation is anticipated to
require more continuous capital expenditures and a less cyclical launch cadence than historic trends.
Currently, we see no evidence of a change in the cyclical nature of demand for heavy lift launch and,
for this reason, have included a cyclical component in our demand forecast.
      Whether the launch destination is GEO or LEO, we assess that commercial buyers will continue
to prioritize three general criteria when selecting a launch provider: reliability/insurability, scheduling
flexibility, and launch vehicle lift/performance.17 Commercial buyers value reliable, flexible, and
targeted launch vehicles and are “willing to pay a premium for improvements in these three areas.”18
The last four years have only heightened this willingness, and launch orders indicate that buyers are
highly motivated to keep suppliers in (or help a second launch provider enter) the addressable market
to provide greater assurance of scheduling flexibility. As we noted in the 2019 assessment, being
certified as an NSS-certified launch provider can affect these areas and affect a provider’s ability to
succeed on the addressable markets. However, while NSS certification may be a testament to a launch
vehicle’s reliability, it may also force a launch manufacturer into designing a launch vehicle that is
suboptimal for the addressable market.

The Status of NSS Launch Contract Awards
    The NSS Phase 2 procurement competitively awarded firm, fixed-price, indefinite delivery
requirements contracts to two providers, SpaceX and ULA. The ordering period was five years
(FY 2020–FY 2024), with a 60/40 split between the launch providers, with ULA selected for the
60-percent share and Space X selected for the 40-percent share. The projected NSS manifest was
approximately 34 missions over the five-year period.
    As of February 2024, the USSF has completed the Phase 2 awards. Nominally, awards are made
two years before expected launch. In FY 2020, three missions were awarded, with two to ULA and
one to SpaceX. Despite nominal launch dates in 2022, only two have launched. USSF-67 was
launched on a Falcon Heavy in January 2023. USSF-51, the ULA mission, was originally planned as
the first Vulcan NSS launch. It was later moved to an Atlas V vehicle, which is allowed under the
terms of the Phase 2 contract, and launched on July 30, 2024. The third launch is for USSF-106 and
is now scheduled for late 2024 as the first NSS Vulcan launch, assuming certification requirements
have been completed by then.24 In FY 2021, four missions were awarded: two to ULA’s Vulcan and
two to SpaceX. None of these has launched. However, the Space Development Agency bought two
additional SpaceX launches in FY 2021 outside the NSS contract for its Tranche 0 Lane 1–Type
payloads; both launches were completed successfully in 2023.25 In FY 2022, eight missions were
awarded for launch in 2024 or later: five to ULA and three to SpaceX; as of August 2024, two have
launched. The FY 2023 award was 12 missions, split evenly between the firms. The FY 2024 award is
21 missions, with 11 to ULA and ten to SpaceX.
    Of the 48 Phase 2 missions awarded, 14 are equivalent in risk to those defined as Lane 1—that is,
relatively risk-tolerant payloads designed for rapid acquisition and deployment. These missions would
be poorly served if they are forced to wait for certification of the Vulcan launch vehicle, and all but
three were awarded to the already NSS-certified SpaceX Falcon 9. Of the remaining 34 missions,
21 were awarded to ULA. These awards were made even though Vulcan had yet to demonstrate a
successful first launch at the time of the awards. A successful first launch was accomplished in January
2024. A second successful launch is needed to complete NSS certification activities, and this is
anticipated to occur mid-September 2024. The implications of this acquisition decision are a major
topic of discussion in Chapter 4.

Chapter 4

Assessing the Impact of NSS Launch
Acquisition Decisions


    This chapter examines the ramifications of the Phase 2 launch acquisition decision, as well as
more recent decisions regarding the Phase 3 NSS launch acquisition strategy on U.S. launch
providers, on the NSS market, and on the global addressable market. We close the chapter with a
discussion of factors that may affect the future resilience of NSS launches.


The Impact of NSS Acquisition Decisions on U.S. Launch
Providers
    In this section, we discuss each of the current and potential U.S. heavy lift launch providers and
the changes in its business case or market position over the past four years. Where we assess that
USSF decisions regarding the NSS market have affected a provider, we highlight that impact.

ULA experience delays in the development of the Vulcan launch vehicle. One NSS Phase 2 mission
was moved to an Atlas V under that provision, and the last Atlas V NSS mission (USSF-51)

The Impact of NSS Acquisition Decisions on the Global
Addressable Market
     To understand the impact of NSS acquisition decisions on U.S. providers that compete in the
global heavy lift launch market, we expand our analysis with the assumption that Arianespace and one
other foreign launch provider successfully bring a new launch vehicle to market. Given SpaceX’s
current monopoly position, there is a significant incentive to bring new vehicles into the global market;
and China, in particular, has several existing vehicles with proven reliability. Over the past two years,
China has matured both the Long March 5 and Long March 7, but has devoted all Long March 3, 5,
and 7 production to its domestic market. Japan does not actively compete in the addressable market
but is accepting launch contracts. Unfortunately, the initial launch of the H-III was not a success.
India intends to compete, has proven launch vehicles, and has executed a handful of addressable
launches. However, based on our analysis of the market, we assess that India has not yet crossed the
chasm.39 Russia, with historically proven expertise in launch, continues to supply those customers in
the addressable global market who do not recognize sanctions imposed in response to its invasion of
Ukraine.
     Our analysis is identical to that performed for the 2019 assessment, but with updated assumptions
regarding the year that new vehicles will be brought to market. For U.S. firms, we include the planned
capacity from SpaceX’s Falcon family of launch vehicles, along with planned capacity from the
following new entrants: Antares 330, Neutron, New Glenn, Terran R, and Vulcan. For each of

Launch Resiliency Considerations
     For this 2023 update, we were also asked to take a preliminary look at resiliency concerns related
to U.S. launch sites. Resiliency is defined as the ability to continue to provide services for NSS launch
when needed by the nation in the event of natural catastrophe or adversary attack. The RAND team
had last examined this topic in an FY 2019 study, and our first step was to review that prior work to
determine if there were any significant events in the intervening years, such as a loss of existing
capacity or an unexpected increase in demand for launch site services, that would change those
findings and recommendations.43 We did not find any.
     We then widened our scope to consider not just the resilience of the launch sites but that of the
critical resources needed for launch. Table 4.1 provides information on key resources needed by
current and new entrant U.S. launch vehicles. As demonstrated in the table, diversity of suppliers does
not always lead to diversification of the key resources needed for launch. While the mutual dependence
of both New Glenn and Vulcan on the BE-4 engine is widely known, other common supply chain
dependencies are more difficult to ascertain due to a lack of public data regarding the supply chains
for these launch vehicles. One item we sought to understand in our discussions with providers was
whether there is a similar mutual reliance on 3D printing technology or supplies. While many of the
newer launch vehicles do rely, or plan to rely, on 3D printing of engines, we observe that no de facto
standard has emerged for the tools or materials needed in the 3D printing of engines. Therefore, it is
our assessment that there is sufficient diversity in the practice of 3D printing that common-cause
failures or supply shortages are unlikely to affect NSS launch in the next five years. As this is a quickly
evolving area of manufacture, this assessment may not be valid a year from now.

     We also looked at the types of fuel needed for each launch vehicle. All current and potential NSS

     Liquid fuels commonly used in launch vehicles include RP-1, methane or LNG, and LH2. RP-1
is a type of kerosene that is highly refined for use in rocket engines, and its production requires
specialized refineries to achieve the necessary level of purity. However, there are currently no
anticipated shortages for these fuels in relation to the current vehicles. Moreover, the newest launch
vehicles will transition to methane or LNG (which is between 85 and 95 percent methane and
5–15 percent ethane, propane, butane, and nitrogen).46 Liquid methane offers easier handling and
storage than RP-1, and nontoxicity, and can be used for a wide range of rocket applications.47 The
LNG supply chain is well established internationally, and the United States produces all the gas that it
consumes, exporting the remainder. U.S. production capacity has been steadily growing over the last
decade and is projected to continue growing in the near and long terms.48 However, there are limited
data available characterizing the methane supply infrastructure needed to support increased launch
demand, and we recommend that the USSF assess the resilience of that infrastructure as it matures.
     LH2 utilization as a fuel in NSS launch vehicles is exclusively limited to the upper stages. While
the utilization of LH2 for rocket applications seems to be decreasing, its utilization is increasing for
other applications—particularly in fuel cell vehicles.49 In the near term we do not anticipate LH2
supply shortages that could disrupt the provider’s planned launch cadence.

Summary
    This chapter has provided in-depth descriptions of the changes on the heavy lift launch market
over the past four years. In general, the NSS Phase 2 acquisition decisions had a significant impact on
both the U.S. providers who were selected and those who were not. Furthermore, delays in bringing
new vehicles to market and SpaceX’s monopoly position have incentivized additional firms to seek to
enter the heavy lift launch market. We assess that the structure of the NSS Phase 3 contract, with its
allowance for unrestricted competition for Lane 1 launches, has been a factor in encouraging at least
some of these new entrants. The most significant impact in the past four years, however, is the backlog
of NSS launches on ULA’s Vulcan manifest, as we will discuss in Chapter 5.

Findings and Recommendations


       In this chapter, we present our key findings and recommendations.


Key Findings
     The average yearly addressable launch demand for heavy lift launch has remained relatively
unchanged since the RAND team’s 2019 assessment.1 Demand rebounded in 2022 after a dismal two
years in 2020 and 2021 and, while 2023 was better than average, the number of addressable launches
made that year did not reach the postpandemic high seen in 2022. Demand is diversifying in terms of
both the types of payloads being launched and their orbital destination. We expect to see slight overall
growth in the next five years, driven by earth and space science payloads.
     There are currently few viable competitors in the global addressable launch market; SpaceX’s
Falcon family of vehicles dominates, with a 70-percent share of the addressable demand in 2022 and a
92-percent share in 2023 (compared with a 40-percent share in 2019). Soyuz had been holding an
equal or greater share to SpaceX’s Falcon; that share was lost due to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in
early 2022. International competitors that we thought might contend for market share have not; the
reasons are varied. China’s Long March family of vehicles appear to have been withdrawn from the
international addressable market and now serve China’s domestic demand only. Both the EU and
Japan have retired their current launch vehicles but have either failed in their attempts to launch new
vehicles (Japan’s H-III) or delayed such an attempt (the EU’s Ariane 6).
     Given the current lack of competitors, several U.S. firms are seeking to enter the heavy lift launch
market. However, in an era when demand is essentially flat, we expect rapid consolidation in suppliers
should any become successful. While new entrants may help to diversify the overall U.S. industrial
base of launch providers, we caution that there are shared common resources (fuel, as well as
geographic concentration of launch complexes) that limit the impact of that diversity on overall
mission resilience.
     One of the largest impacts NSS decisions have had on the heavy lift launch market in recent years
is the selection of SpaceX’s Falcon family and ULA’s Vulcan as NSS launch providers under the
Phase 2 acquisition. That decision, and the backlog of 25 NSS missions that has built up while
Vulcan works toward a successful completion of NSS certification activities, is likely to constrain
Vulcan supply in the addressable market for several years. The last NSS payloads awarded under the
Phase 2 contract are likely to experience a two-and-one-half-year delay even if all Vulcan production is

Recommendations
     As recommended in the 2019 launch market assessment, the USSF should make prudent
preparations for a future with only two U.S. providers of NSS certified heavy lift launch, at least one
of which may have little support from the addressable marketplace. It is unlikely that other nations
will allow U.S. firms to dominate the addressable market, and we do not foresee sufficient growth in
the addressable or in U.S. nonaddressable demand to support more than two U.S.-based launch
providers in the long term.
     Despite the above recognition that the markets will not support more than two U.S. firms over the
long term, we recommend that the USSF continue to provide support to enable as many U.S. launch
providers as possible to enter the markets. We assess that the NSS Phase 3 launch contract, which
allows for unrestricted competition for NSS Lane 1 missions, does this. By removing restrictions on
competition for these missions, the USSF provides an incentive for U.S. firms to enter the heavy lift
launch market without unnecessarily distorting that market. Regarding the USSF’s decision to select
up to three providers for NSS Lane 2 missions, we note that our analysis is largely agnostic. Doing so
will certainly lead to earlier market consolidation, but that consolidation would occur regardless.
Doing so may also deter foreign competitors from entering the market, but we see no evidence that
foreign competitors factor that into their decisions.
     The USSF has recognized the risks of adding to backlogs of firms that have not yet been certified
for NSS launch and has mitigated that risk in the terms and conditions of the Phase 3 proposed
contracts. While we are confident this step will allow the USSF to avoid worsening the existing
situation, as noted above, the realized risk of the Vulcan Phase 2 mission backlog will affect Vulcan’s
participation in the addressable market for several years.
     Finally, given the concentration of volatile fuels at Cape Canaveral, in particular, the USSF may
wish to consider adding a diversity of launch sites or of fuel sources as a secondary consideration in
future launch acquisition source selections to promote mission resilience.

Data Sources
    The RAND-developed heavy lift dataset documented in the 2019 report is the basis for the
historic launch data used in our assessment.1 We updated the dataset for the years 2020–2023 for this

secondary verification.3 Where discrepancies exist between these sources, we turned to open-source

methodology in determining the categorization, purpose, class of orbit, and operating entity is identical
to that used in the 2019 assessment.


Analysis Overview
    We project futures of the supply and demand of NSS and global addressable launch markets using
Monte Carlo simulations, which are designed to estimate the distribution of a variable by letting it
mimic the behavior of drawing many random samples from a specified known distribution. The
benefit of simulating a distribution of many values (instead of a single value) is that distributions can
be summarized to understand their expected value (typically, the arithmetic mean), their uncertainty
(approximated by their variance), or their range (which we capture by looking at percentiles).
    Sometimes researchers are particularly concerned with capturing their uncertainty in a variable’s
distribution. Because Monte Carlo simulations have no limitations on what distributions can be
chosen, researchers can fix any distribution’s hyperparameters to maximally capture their uncertainty.
For our analysis, we draw randomly from continuous uniform distributions for the variables we are
uncertain of. Because the uniform distribution has a variance that is proportional to the range of the
distribution, we capture our uncertainty by adjusting the range of the distribution.4 We start by
simulating variables (e.g., percentage of NSS launches delayed, or supplier capacity) with unique
distributions—thereby assuming they behave independently. We then use these simulated variables to

T
                he U.S. military depends on space-based assets for strategic and tactical
                communications, for collection of imagery over contested areas, for warning
                of missile launches, and for precision navigation and timing. The ability to
                replenish those assets in a timely fashion may affect overall force readiness.
                The U.S. Space Force (USSF) is required to sustain at least two viable U.S.-
based launch service providers for national security space (NSS) payloads. In 2019, a
RAND team assessed the global heavy lift launch market to understand the impact of
NSS acquisition decisions on that market. Space Exploration Technologies Corporation
(SpaceX) and United Launch Alliance (ULA) are the current NSS launch providers, having
been selected for the Phase 2 launch contracts awarded between fiscal years 2020 and
2024. Although ULA is retiring its prior NSS-certified launch vehicles, its new vehicle has
yet to complete NSS certification, leaving the USSF temporarily reliant on SpaceX as the
only NSS-certified launch service provider.

The RAND team was asked to update its assessment of the launch markets to better
understand how the USSF might shape future acquisition strategies or prepare for
operational risks introduced by past acquisition decisions. The authors examine historical
and projected levels of supply and demand in the global and NSS heavy lift launch
markets. They forecast demand over the next ten years (2024–2033) and explore how
assumptions regarding future market dynamics might affect the USSF’s ability to meet
NSS demand and sustain at least two certified suppliers.
